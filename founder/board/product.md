# Product lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

Stage scores: Stage 1 (alloys) 4/10. Stage 2 (used cars) 6/10. Stage 3 (prestige hire) 2/10. Overall 4/10.

## What is strong
- Stage 2 has the seed of a real product. A history check, fresh MOT and inspection report handed to a nervous first-car buyer or parent is an experience, not just a car. That is the only part of the plan that starts from the customer.
- Showing the buyer the inspection report is the right kind of craft. Honest detail the buyer can see and trust is what a £2,000-£5,000 buyer wants.
- Chauffeur-driven before self-drive in Stage 3 is a sensible cut. The founder controls the experience and the risk.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. No focus. Three different businesses (parts, used cars, event hire) with three different customers, skills and legal regimes, run by one part-time person. Nothing is "the one thing". The pitch needs a paragraph, so the product is not clear yet. Stage 3 in particular has no price, no described experience and no named customer need; it is a wish, not a product.
2. Stage 1 and Stage 2 are not products yet, they are trades. Flipping alloys is buy low, sell high, with no stated experience for the buyer. Flipping cars is the same unless the founder owns the whole experience: sourcing, prep, listing, viewing, handover, paperwork and what happens when the car develops a fault in week two. The brief says nothing about aftersales, warranty or complaints. That is the seam where a £3,000 car business breaks and where a cheap-car buyer feels cheated.
3. Hidden quality is unproven. The inspection report and prep only count if the founder can actually judge and fix cars, or has someone who can. The brief does not say who does the mechanical inspection or the prep, or to what standard. If the report is a formality, the whole Stage 2 promise is a lie that shows up after the sale.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Say what GCC is in one sentence, for one customer. What is the one thing you must do brilliantly?
- Walk me through the first five minutes a buyer spends with one of your cars: the listing, the message, the viewing, the report, the handover. What do they feel?
- Who does the inspection and prep: you, a garage, a mobile mechanic? What is your own mechanical skill, honestly? (Open question, only the founder can answer.)
- What happens when a buyer rings you in week two with a fault? What do you promise in writing?
- What would you cut? If you could only keep one stage, which, and why not just do that one?
- Stage 3: what does a customer actually get, and why would they pick you over an established operator? What is the price? (Open question. /founder-competitors answers who else sells this; /founder-consumer answers what buyers want and will pay; /founder-cfo answers whether the price works.)
- What do alloys have to do with a car buyer's experience at all? Is Stage 1 anything but a way to raise cash?
- Do you know how many hours a week you can give this around Tesco shifts, and what that does to response time and handover quality? (Open question for the founder; /founder-cfo for what the hours are worth.)
- Starting capital is not stated. How much is it? (Open question; /founder-cfo.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
- Cut to one product, Stage 2, written as one sentence with one named customer. Treat Stage 1 as a short funding step with an end date, and park Stage 3 until Stage 2 has a track record. Do not run all three at once.
- Define the full Stage 2 experience on paper before buying a car: listing, viewing, report, handover, paperwork, aftersales promise. Name every handoff and who owns it.
- Name who inspects and preps each car and to what standard, and show a sample inspection report the buyer would receive.
- Write the week-two fault policy before the first sale.
- Prove it small: sell the first few cars and show the buyers' actual experience before any talk of relocating or hire. Use /founder-consumer to test what Stage 2 buyers actually value and /founder-competitors to see what other small dealers already offer.
- Legal questions raised in the brief (trader versus private-seller consumer law, motor trade insurance, hire-and-reward insurance and licensing) are product conditions too, because they decide what you can promise. Get them answered before selling, not after.
