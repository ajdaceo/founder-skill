## 0. Findings that change the plan (read first)

1. **The CRP requirement is narrower than "all public tenders".** PPN 006 binds central government bodies for contracts above £5 million per year. The NHS extends it "proportionately" to all new procurements. Marketing that tells small companies a CRP is a general legal requirement would be misleading (see 2.6, 6.3).
2. **Emissions figures are not scored.** A CRP passes or fails on structure (commitment, all required scopes, declaration, environmental measures, reporting period, publication). A wrong number is less likely to lose a tender than a missing section. Automated checks should target the published fail conditions (see 1.5, 7).
3. **"Minimal personal involvement" is the weak point**, for four separate reasons: unlimited personal liability as a sole trader (5.6); insurers' focus on human supervision of AI services and at least one relevant published policy wording that excludes "computer or digital technology error" (3.4); Anthropic's commercial terms put the human-review decision on the customer (6.2); the director's signed declaration relies on the plan being right (1.4).
4. **Cold email is lawful only to corporate subscribers**, and the list must exclude sole traders and ordinary partnerships. Named addresses bring UK GDPR duties. Anthropic's Usage Policy prohibits generating or distributing spam and AI personas that pose as human (4, 6.2).

## 2. Liability if a figure is wrong

### 2.1 The contract with a limited company is B2B: Consumer Rights Act 2015 does not apply
- URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/1
- Quote: "This Part applies where there is an agreement between a trader and a consumer for the trader to supply goods, digital content or services"
- URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/2
- Quote: "“Consumer” means an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside that individual's trade, business, craft or profession."
- So the CRA is business-to-consumer only. A limited company is not an "individual". Selling to limited companies only keeps the service outside the CRA. (If a sole-trader client were ever accepted, the position is less clean. Solicitor.)

### 2.2 Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982: implied duty of reasonable care and skill (B2B)
- URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1982/29/section/13
- Quote: "In a [relevant contract for the supply of a service] where the supplier is acting in the course of a business, there is an implied term that the supplier will carry out the service with reasonable care and skill."
- It can be varied by contract, subject to UCTA. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1982/29/section/16
- Quote: "it may (subject to subsection (2) below and the 1977 Act) be negatived or varied by express agreement"
- Extent shown on legislation.gov.uk for ss.12, 13, 16 is "E+W+N.I.". Scotland relies on common law for the equivalent term and on Part II of UCTA. UNCONFIRMED detail; Scottish solicitor if the owner or clients are in Scotland.
- Using AI does not lower the standard. No source says it does; the insurer and Anthropic sources in 3.4 and 6.2 say responsibility stays with the provider. UNCONFIRMED as a statement of case law.

### 2.3 Realistic heads of claim
- Breach of contract (express terms such as "compliant with PPN 006", plus the s.13 implied term).
- Negligence / negligent misstatement (concurrent duty in tort). General common-law principle; not sourced to an opened primary authority. UNCONFIRMED.
- Misrepresentation, for statements made before the contract (for example on the website or in a cold email). URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1967/7/section/2
- Quote: "that person shall be so liable notwithstanding that the misrepresentation was not made fraudulently, unless he proves that he had reasonable ground to believe and did believe up to the time the contract was made the facts represented were true."
- What the loss would be: the client's exposure is failing the condition of participation (1.5) or, in a bad case, discretionary exclusion. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/schedule/7 (para 13, in force 24.2.2025)
- Quote: "A supplier might act improperly in relation to a procurement by— ... (b)providing information that is incomplete, inaccurate or misleading;" (ground applies only if "in so doing, the supplier put itself at an unfair advantage in relation to the award of a public contract")
- A lost-tender claim against the service provider would be for loss of profit or loss of chance on a contract that, where PPN 006 applies, is worth over £5 million a year. That is wildly disproportionate to a £249 fee, which is why the cap and insurance matter. Whether such loss is recoverable (causation, remoteness, loss of chance, the client's own director having signed the declaration) is fact-specific. UNCONFIRMED; solicitor.
- Mitigating facts from the policy itself: figures are "for information only" (1.5); the supplier, not the preparer, self-declares and is told to "ensure that CRPs produced by third parties on their behalf still meet the requirements of this policy" (1.3).

### 2.4 Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977: limits on limiting liability between businesses
- Negligence. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/50/section/2
- Quote: "In the case of other loss or damage, a person cannot so exclude or restrict his liability for negligence except in so far as the term or notice satisfies the requirement of reasonableness."
- Standard terms. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/50/section/3
- Quote: "This section applies as between contracting parties where one of them deals ... on the other’s written standard terms of business." and the other party cannot "when himself in breach of contract, exclude or restrict any liability of his in respect of the breach ... except in so far as ... the contract term satisfies the requirement of reasonableness."
- Reasonableness test. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/50/section/11
- Quote (s.11(1)): "the term shall have been a fair and reasonable one to be included having regard to the circumstances which were, or ought reasonably to have been, known to or in the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made."
- Quote (s.11(4), money caps): "regard shall be had in particular ... to— (a)the resources which he could expect to be available to him for the purpose of meeting the liability should it arise; and (b)how far it was open to him to cover himself by insurance."
- Quote (s.11(5)): "It is for those claiming that a contract term or notice satisfies the requirement of reasonableness to show that it does."
- Schedule 2 guidelines. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/50/schedule/2
- Quote: "(a)the strength of the bargaining positions of the parties relative to each other ... (c)whether the customer knew or ought reasonably to have known of the existence and the extent of the term"
- Misrepresentation exclusions face the same test. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1967/7/section/3
- Quote: "that term shall be of no effect except in so far as it satisfies the requirement of reasonableness as stated in section 11(1) of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977"

### 2.5 Is a cap at the fee paid enforceable? Sometimes; it is not safe to assume
- Upheld in principle between businesses. Goodlife Foods Ltd v Hall Fire Protection Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 1371. URL: https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2018/1371
- Quote (para 68): "the courts have upheld clauses in which the subcontractor or supplier has limited its liability to the contract sum, or another amount which was tiny compared to the damages claimed."
- Struck down where it sat badly with insurance. Trustees of Ampleforth Abbey Trust v Turner & Townsend Project Management Ltd [2012] EWHC 2137 (TCC). URL: https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2012/2137
- Quote (para 201): "I ... hold that the limitation clause was unreasonable. The central factor that leads me to that decision is that the contract imposed on TTPM an obligation to take out professional indemnity insurance to a level of £10 million. ... Yet the limitation clause would result in a limit of liability equal to the fees paid to TTPM, which is £111,321"
- Reading across (my inference, not a holding): a £249 cap from an uninsured sole trader selling a low-price templated product to a company that could have used a free tool has arguments in its favour (price, alternatives, clear notice). A £249 cap from a provider who advertises £1 million of PI cover is exposed to the Ampleforth reasoning. A cap set at, or linked to, the insured amount or a stated multiple of the fee is easier to defend. Drafting and prominence matter (Goodlife turned partly on the clause being flagged up front). Solicitor needed for the actual terms; UNCONFIRMED as a prediction.
- Cannot exclude at all: death or personal injury from negligence (s.2(1), quoted on the s.2 page: "cannot ... exclude or restrict his liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence"). Fraud cannot be excluded at common law (UNCONFIRMED, not sourced).

### 2.6 Greenwashing exposure for the service provider (as opposed to the client)
- DMCCA 2024 unfair commercial practices are consumer-facing. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/section/225
- Quote: "“commercial practice” means an act or omission by a trader relating to the promotion or supply of— (a)the trader’s product to a consumer, (b)another trader’s product to a consumer, or (c)a consumer’s product to the trader or another person" and "“consumer” means an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside the individual’s business"
- So a provider selling only to limited companies is not directly within the DMCCA unfair-commercial-practices regime for its own sales. CMA's DMCCA guidance page (CMA207), last updated 18 November 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207
- B2B marketing is covered by the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/1276/regulation/3
- Quote: "Advertising which is misleading is prohibited." It is misleading if it "deceives or is likely to deceive the traders to whom it is addressed or whom it reaches; and by reason of its deceptive nature, is likely to affect their economic behaviour"; relevant features include "fitness for purpose of the product" and "results to be expected from use of the product".
- CMA Green Claims guidance on B2B. URL: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-claims-code-making-environmental-claims/environmental-claims-on-goods-and-services
- Quote: "This guidance also applies (to a more limited extent) to businesses marketing to other businesses." and "The legal framework regulating business-to-business marketing is less comprehensive than for business-to-consumer commercial practices. It prohibits misleading advertising and misleading comparative advertising."
- Note: that CMA page still describes itself as "based on consumer protection rules under the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008", i.e. it has not been rewritten for the DMCCA. Treat with care.
- ASA / CAP Code applies to B2B emails and the provider's own website. URL: https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_folder/scope-of-the-code.html
- Quote: "advertisements in newspapers, magazines, brochures, leaflets, circulars, mailings, e-mails ..." and "a consumer is anyone who is likely to see a given marketing communication, whether in the course of business or not"
- CAP rule 3.1 (https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html): "Marketing communications must not materially mislead or be likely to do so." Rule 3.7: "marketers must hold documentary evidence to prove claims that consumers are likely to regard as objective"
- CAP section 11 (https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/11.html): "The basis of environmental claims must be clear. Unqualified claims could mislead if they omit material information."
- Where the real risk sits for the provider: (a) its own claims, such as "guaranteed compliant", "win more tenders", "required by law", "verified"; (b) net-zero or reduction statements it drafts that the client then publishes on its website. The client is the party making that published claim; the provider's exposure for it is contractual (2.3), not regulatory. UNCONFIRMED whether a regulator would pursue the drafter; no source found either way.

### 3.3 What PI would and would not answer
- Hiscox "Professional indemnity for green consultants" wording, reference "WD-PROF-UK-GRN-AG(3) 15589 01/21", linked from the Hiscox environmental consultants page on the research date. URL: https://www.hiscox.co.uk/sites/default/files/documents/2021-05/15589_WD-PROF-UK-GRN-AG.pdf

- Contractual promises beyond the general law are excluded, quote: "any liability under any contract which is greater than the liability you would have at law without the contract." So a "guaranteed compliant / guaranteed to pass" promise would be uninsured under this wording.

### 3.4 Do insurers exclude or restrict AI-generated advice? Mixed, and one concrete problem found
- No AI-specific words appear in that Hiscox green-consultants wording. But it excludes, quote: "We will not make any payment for any claim or part of a claim or loss directly or indirectly due to: ... 12. or contributed to by, resulting from or in connection with any computer or digital technology error."
- Definition, quote: "Computer or digital technology error: Any negligent act, error or omission by anyone in the: 1. creation, handling, entry, modification or maintenance of; or 2. on-going operation, maintenance ... or development of; any computer or digital technology." and "Computer or digital technology: Any programs, computer network, hardware, software ... or cloud computing services."
- The matching summary (https://www.hiscox.co.uk/sites/default/files/documents/2021-05/16981_PS-PROF-UK-GRN-AG.pdf) lists as a significant exclusion: "any negligent act, error or omission in the operation or maintenance of computer or digital technology such as development, installation, patching or upgrading"
- Why it matters: an error traced to how the owner built or ran an automated AI pipeline could be argued to fall inside that exclusion. Whether it would is a coverage question for the insurer. UNCONFIRMED; ask in writing before buying.
- Broker view. PolicyBee, "How to make sure you have insurance for AI risks", dated 30/04/2026. URL: https://www.policybee.co.uk/blog/insurance-for-ai-risks
- Quote: "So, you should be covered. However, always double-check your policy wording. Your insurer may include an exclusion for AI-related claims."
- Insurance-law view. DAC Beachcroft, "Published 28 September 2026". URL: https://www.dacbeachcroft.com/en/What-we-think/Professional-indemnity-insurance-risk-of-AI-hallucinations
- Quote: "Professional indemnity policies currently tend not to explicitly cover AI-related errors or omissions, although these claims are likely to be analysed as conventional negligence claims. Provided the claim falls within the definition of professional services and no exclusion applies, many PI policies may respond irrespective of whether AI formed part of the factual background."
- Quote: "insurers are likely to focus closely, before inception, on how AI-enabled services are supervised and disclosed."
- Quote: "The key risk for firms is not simply that AI may produce inaccurate output, but that such output may be relied upon without adequate human review or clear responsibility for verification."
- Affirmative AI cover exists in one product line (trade press, in partnership with Hiscox, 03 Jul 2025). URL: https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/professional-liability/hiscox-launches-first-affirmative-ai-liability-cover-in-uk-market-540863.aspx

Get written confirmation that AI-drafted deliverables are within the insured "business activity" and that no technology-error or AI exclusion bites. Insurer/broker needed.

## 4. Marketing by email (PECR and UK GDPR as at late 2026)

### 4.1 Status of the law after the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (DUAA)
- ICO overview, "Latest updates - 19 June 2026". URL: https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/legislation-we-cover/data-use-and-access-act-2025/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-what-does-it-mean-for-organisations/
- Quote: "all data protection provisions in the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 are now in force."
- Quote: "The DUAA amends, but does not replace, the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR), the Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA) and the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR)."
- What changed that is relevant here:
  - Direct marketing named as a possible legitimate interest. Quote: "it clarifies that direct marketing can be a legitimate interest"
  - New complaints duty. Quote: "the DUAA requires you to take steps to help people who want to make complaints about how you use their personal information, such as providing an electronic complaints form. You also have to acknowledge complaints within 30 days and respond to them ‘without undue delay’."
  - PECR penalties raised to UK GDPR levels. URL: https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/legislation-we-cover/data-use-and-access-act-2025/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-summary-of-the-changes/privacy-and-electronic-communications/ Quote: "It brings the enforcement powers under PECR into line with UK GDPR, so that enforcement mechanisms and penalties are the same in most cases." (The cash maximum, commonly given as £17.5 million or 4% of turnover, was not opened in a primary source: UNCONFIRMED.)
  - New soft opt-in is for charities only (PECR reg 22(3A), in force 5.2.2026). Not available to this business.
- The corporate-subscriber position did not change (4.2).

### 4.2 Unsolicited marketing email to limited companies and LLPs: lawful under PECR without consent
- PECR reg 22 applies only to individual subscribers. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/regulation/22 (text shows DUAA amendments "inserted (5.2.2026)")
- Quote: "This regulation applies to the transmission of unsolicited communications by means of electronic mail to individual subscribers."
- ICO guidance on direct marketing using electronic mail, "Latest updates - 28 April 2026". URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/
- Quote: "You can send unsolicited electronic mail marketing to corporate subscribers without consent or a soft opt-in."
- Who counts. URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/key-concepts-for-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/
- Quote: "individual subscribers (people, sole traders, ordinary partnerships); and corporate subscribers (organisations with their own legal personality, for example limited companies, LLPs and Scottish partnerships)."
- ICO B2B guidance (flagged by ICO as "under review" because of DUAA). URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/
- Quote: "So, for example, the email address or telephone number of an employee at a corporate body would constitute a corporate subscriber for the purposes of PECR because the ‘subscriber’ is their employer."
- Opt-outs from companies. Quote: "PECR does not say that you must comply with a corporate subscriber’s opt-out in the context of electronic mail. ... Therefore, you should comply with a corporate subscriber’s opt-out request."
- The sender is responsible even when a platform sends. Quote (electronic mail guidance): "using a webmail service or bulk-email platform does not normally make that service responsible. ... You remain responsible for complying with PECR."

### 4.3 Sole traders and ordinary partnerships: consent (or soft opt-in) needed, so they must be kept off the cold list
- ICO B2B guidance quote: "Because sole traders and some partnerships are treated by PECR as individual subscribers you can only market them by electronic mail if they have specifically consented, or the ‘soft opt-in’ applies."
- Quote: "If you are unsure whether the contact details belong to an individual subscriber or a corporate subscriber this puts you at risk of breaching PECR. To mitigate that risk you should treat the details as belonging to an individual subscriber"
- Public availability is not consent. Electronic mail guidance quote: "Just because someone’s contact details are publicly available, it doesn’t mean they’ve consented to your direct marketing."
- Soft opt-in cannot be used for bought lists. Quote: "you must not use the soft opt-ins to send unsolicited electronic mail marketing to people on a bought-in marketing list."
- Personal addresses used by company staff are also risky. B2B guidance quote: the position differs if addresses "belong to sole traders or because the delegate chose to use their personal email address instead of their work one."
- Practical consequence: verify each target against Companies House as a live limited company or LLP, use the company's own domain address, and drop gmail-type addresses.

### 4.4 What each marketing email must contain or do
- PECR reg 23. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/regulation/23
- Quote: must not send "(a)where the identity of the person on whose behalf the communication has been sent has been disguised or concealed; ... (b)where a valid address to which the recipient of the communication may send a request that such communications cease has not been provided; (c)where that electronic mail would contravene regulation 7 of the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002"
- ICO: these apply to corporate recipients too. Quote: "You must not disguise or hide your identity in messages to either type of subscriber. You must provide a valid contact address for recipients to opt out or unsubscribe."
- Electronic Commerce Regulations 2002 reg 7. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/7
- Quote: "(a)be clearly identifiable as a commercial communication; (b)clearly identify the person on whose behalf the commercial communication is made"
- Reg 8. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/2013/regulation/8
- Quote: "any unsolicited commercial communication sent by him by electronic mail is clearly and unambiguously identifiable as such as soon as it is received."
- Sole trader using a trading name must give own name and a service address (see 5.4).
- Where the address is a named person: the right to object and a link to the privacy notice (4.5).
- Tracking pixels are regulated separately. ICO quote: "If the email includes tracking pixels, you must comply with the rules on storage and access technologies." Simplest course: no open-tracking pixels in cold email.
- Checklist for every message: real sender name plus trading name; postal address for service; a working reply or unsubscribe route; obviously a sales email from the subject line and first lines; why you are writing to them; privacy notice link; right-to-object line if a named person.

### 4.5 Named individual at a company (jane@company.co.uk): UK GDPR applies
- ICO B2B guidance quote: "If you can identify an individual either directly or indirectly it will constitute personal data even if they are acting in their business capacity."
- Worked example quote: "Because the HR director’s email address constitutes a corporate subscriber for the purposes of PECR the rules on consent and the soft opt-in do not apply. However the HR director’s email address identifies them by name"
- Lawful basis: legitimate interests is the usual one, with an assessment. Quote: "If PECR does not require consent, in many cases it is likely that legitimate interests will be the appropriate lawful basis. But there is no absolute rule and you need to apply the legitimate interests three-part test"
- ICO legitimate interests guidance. URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/legitimate-interests/when-can-we-rely-on-legitimate-interests/
- Quote: "Article 6(11)(a) of the UK GDPR says: ... (a) processing that is necessary for the purposes of direct marketing" and "This means that direct marketing "may" be a legitimate interest. However, the UK GDPR doesn’t say that direct marketing always constitutes a legitimate interest."
- Quote: "Business contacts are more likely to reasonably expect you to use their personal information in a business context. Such use is less likely to have a significant impact on them personally."
- Right to object is absolute. URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-object/ ("Latest updates - 2 November 2026")
- Quote: "This is an absolute right and there are no exemptions or grounds for you to refuse."
- Quote: "You should inform people of their right to object as soon as possible, and must inform them no later than at the point of your first communication with them."
- Quote: "You must respond to an objection without undue delay and at the latest within one month"
- Suppress, do not delete. Quote: "You should not delete a person's information from a suppression list."
- Privacy notice when data came from elsewhere. URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-be-informed/
- Quote: "within a reasonable period of obtaining the personal data and no later than one month; if you use the data to communicate with the individual, at the latest, when the first communication takes place"
- The notice must include "The source of the personal data (if the personal data is not obtained from the individual it relates to)."
- Lower-risk option: write to generic role addresses (info@, sales@, tenders@) at verified limited companies. A generic address that does not identify a person is not personal data on the ICO test quoted above (my inference from that test).
- Not researched: sending prospect names and emails through a US AI provider is an international transfer needing a data processing agreement and transfer safeguards. UNCONFIRMED; check the provider's DPA.

It is lost as soon as personal data is processed for any other purpose (for example named employee commuting or travel data supplied by a client, if held as a controller). ## 7. Is specialist human review of each plan necessary?

- In law: no requirement found. No licensing (1.3); no audit requirement (1.4).
- Under the procurement rules: no. The only mandated review is the client's own. Quotes: "there is no requirement to have your carbon footprint audited" (technical standard para 18); "must be signed off by a director (or equivalent)" (para 25); "Suppliers should ensure that CRPs produced by third parties on their behalf still meet the requirements of this policy" (FAQ Q17).
- Under Anthropic's terms: not mandated for this use on my reading (6.2), but "where human review is appropriate" is the customer's responsibility (D.3).
- For insurance and liability: prudent, and possibly a practical condition of getting cover. Quote (DAC Beachcroft, 3.4): "insurers are likely to focus closely, before inception, on how AI-enabled services are supervised and disclosed." The legal standard is "reasonable care and skill" (2.2) and the footprint "should be conducted to a reasonable level of assurance" (1.4). A provider with no human check at all would find it harder to show reasonable care after an error. UNCONFIRMED as case law; solicitor.

### A proportionate safeguard (my recommendation, built from the sources above)
1. Deterministic arithmetic. Software code, not the language model, multiplies activity data by the DESNZ factor for the correct year, with units checked. The model drafts narrative only.
2. Automated structural checks mapped to the published fail conditions (1.5): net zero commitment and date present; Scope 1, Scope 2 and each of the five required Scope 3 categories reported or an explanation given; reporting period within 18 months and aligned to the financial year; environmental management measures present; declaration, name, job title and date fields present; official template headings intact; publication reminder.
3. Plausibility flags: zero or missing categories; emissions per employee outside a sane range; year-on-year swings; mixed units; electricity without a location-based figure.
4. A client verification step before delivery is final: a one-page input summary the client confirms, and explicit instructions that the director must check and sign.
5. A short human look by the owner (a few minutes per plan against a checklist) on every plan at first, moving to exceptions plus a random sample once error rates are known. Record it. This is the piece insurers ask about.
6. Optional paid specialist spot-check of a sample each quarter, or on any plan a client says is for a specific live tender.
7. Keep an audit trail: inputs, factor version, checks run, who reviewed, what was sent.
8. Do not promise outcomes. No "guaranteed compliant" or "guaranteed to pass" (uninsurable under the wording in 3.3 and risky under 2.3 and 2.6).
